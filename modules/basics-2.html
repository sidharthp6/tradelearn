How Markets Work

Financial markets aren't magic — they're organised systems where buyers and sellers meet, agree on prices, and exchange assets. Understanding how this machinery works gives you a huge edge over traders who treat price movements as random noise.

What is a Financial Market?

A financial market is any organised place or system where people can buy and sell financial assets. Markets exist to serve three crucial functions:

  • Price discovery: Markets aggregate the opinions of millions of buyers and sellers to produce a "fair" price at any given moment.
  • Liquidity: Markets ensure you can buy or sell an asset quickly without dramatically moving its price.
  • Capital allocation: Money flows to the companies and projects people believe will generate the best returns.

Markets can be physical (the trading floor of the NYSE) or electronic (the vast majority of trading today happens via computer networks with no human floor traders involved).

Exchanges and Brokers

You don't trade directly on an exchange as a retail trader — you go through a broker. Here's how the chain works:

  1. You place an order through your broker's platform (app or website).
  2. Your broker routes that order to the appropriate exchange or market maker.
  3. The exchange's order book matches your order with someone on the other side.
  4. The trade executes and both parties exchange the asset and money.
Key Term: Order Book

A real-time, electronic list of all outstanding buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks) for a particular asset, organised by price. The exchange's matching engine pairs up buyers and sellers automatically.

The Bid-Ask Spread

Every asset has two prices at any moment — the bid and the ask:

  • Bid price: the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay.
  • Ask price: the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept.
  • Spread: the difference between ask and bid. This is the cost of trading.
Example — Reading the Spread

A stock shows: Bid $49.96 / Ask $50.02. The spread is $0.06. If you buy at $50.02 (the ask) and immediately sell at $49.96 (the bid), you instantly lose $0.06 per share. You need the price to move more than the spread just to break even — which is why reducing trading costs matters so much.

Liquid assets (heavily traded, like Apple stock or EUR/USD) have very tight spreads. Illiquid assets (small-cap stocks, exotic currency pairs) have wider spreads that eat into your profits.

What Actually Moves Prices?

Price is determined by supply and demand. When more people want to buy than sell, price rises. When more want to sell than buy, price falls. But what drives those buying and selling decisions?

Fundamental Factors

For stocks: company earnings, revenue growth, profit margins, new products, management changes. For currencies: interest rates, inflation, GDP growth. For commodities: weather, supply disruptions, geopolitical events.

Macroeconomic Data

Reports like US Non-Farm Payrolls, CPI (inflation), and central bank interest rate decisions can cause massive, sudden price moves across multiple markets simultaneously.

Sentiment and Speculation

Markets are also driven by human psychology — fear and greed. News, social media, and analyst opinions can move prices dramatically, sometimes regardless of fundamentals. The 2021 GameStop short squeeze is a famous example: retail traders on Reddit drove a struggling company's stock up 1,700% in a month.

Tip: Don't Fight the Market

Many beginners lose money by trading against the current trend because they believe the price "must" turn around. Markets can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent. Always trade with the current market direction, not against it.
