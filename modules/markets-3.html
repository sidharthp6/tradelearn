Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrency is digital money that operates independently of any central bank or government. It is secured by cryptography and recorded on a distributed network called a blockchain. Since Bitcoin's launch in 2009, crypto has grown from a niche experiment into a multi-trillion dollar asset class that commands the attention of retail traders, institutions, and regulators worldwide.

What is Cryptocurrency?

A cryptocurrency is a digital asset designed to work as a medium of exchange. Unlike government-issued currencies (fiat money), no single authority controls it — there is no Federal Reserve of Bitcoin.

The term "crypto" comes from the cryptographic techniques used to secure transactions and control the creation of new units. Every transaction is publicly recorded and permanently stored on the blockchain — making the system transparent and highly resistant to fraud.

Key Term: Cryptography

The practice of securing information using mathematical algorithms. In crypto, cryptography ensures that only the rightful owner of a wallet can authorise transactions, and that no one can counterfeit or double-spend coins.

How Blockchain Works

The blockchain is the technology that makes cryptocurrency possible. Think of it as a public ledger — a record book — that stores every transaction ever made, visible to anyone, and stored simultaneously on thousands of computers around the world.

Here's how a transaction gets recorded:

  1. You initiate a transaction (send Bitcoin to a friend).
  2. That transaction is broadcast to a network of computers (nodes).
  3. Nodes verify the transaction is valid (you actually own the Bitcoin you're sending).
  4. The verified transaction is grouped with others into a "block."
  5. That block is permanently added to the chain of previous blocks — hence "blockchain."

Because thousands of independent nodes each hold a copy of the entire blockchain, there is no central server to hack or shut down. Altering historical records would require simultaneously changing the blockchain on the majority of all nodes worldwide — computationally impossible.

Bitcoin and Altcoins

Bitcoin (BTC) is the first and most famous cryptocurrency, created in 2009 by an anonymous figure (or group) known as Satoshi Nakamoto. Its key properties:

  • Fixed supply: only 21 million Bitcoin will ever exist — scarcity is built into the code.
  • Decentralised: no company, government, or individual controls it.
  • Store of value: often called "digital gold" due to its scarcity and resistance to inflation.
Key Term: Altcoin

Any cryptocurrency that is not Bitcoin. "Alt" stands for alternative. There are over 20,000 altcoins, including major ones like Ethereum, Solana, and Cardano — and thousands of speculative or worthless tokens.

Ethereum (ETH) is the second-largest cryptocurrency and fundamentally different from Bitcoin. It's a programmable blockchain that enables smart contracts — self-executing code that automatically performs actions when conditions are met. This powers DeFi (decentralised finance), NFTs, and thousands of decentralised applications.

Why Crypto is So Volatile

Crypto is notorious for its extreme price swings. Bitcoin has lost 80%+ of its value multiple times, then recovered to new all-time highs. Several factors drive this volatility:

  • Smaller market size: the entire crypto market is still small compared to stocks or forex. Large individual trades move prices more dramatically.
  • 24/7 trading: markets never close. News breaks at 3 AM and prices immediately react — there's no "after-hours" buffer.
  • Speculative nature: much of crypto's price is driven by speculation about future adoption, not current earnings or dividends.
  • Sentiment amplification: social media (Twitter/X, Reddit) and influential figures can cause massive swings within hours.
Tip: Volatility Cuts Both Ways

Crypto's volatility means extraordinary gains are possible — Bitcoin went from $0.01 in 2010 to nearly $109,000 in 2025. But the same volatility means losses can be devastating. Never invest in crypto more than you can afford to lose entirely, and always use risk management techniques from the Basics modules.
