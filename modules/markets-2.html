Forex — Foreign Exchange

The foreign exchange market is the largest financial market on Earth, with over $7.5 trillion traded every single day. It never sleeps — it operates 24 hours a day, five days a week across global banking networks from Sydney to Tokyo to London to New York.

What is Forex?

Forex is simply the exchange of one currency for another. Every time a business imports goods from abroad, a tourist buys foreign currency at the airport, or a central bank intervenes in currency markets — that's forex.

Unlike the stock market, forex has no central exchange. It is an "over-the-counter" (OTC) market, meaning trades happen directly between participants — banks, institutions, brokers, and retail traders — through a global electronic network.

Key Term: Over-the-Counter (OTC)

Trading that happens directly between two parties, without going through a centralised exchange. The forex market is decentralised — there is no single "forex exchange." Instead, multiple banks and brokers provide prices and match trades globally.

Currency Pairs Explained

In forex, you never trade a single currency — you always trade one currency against another. This is expressed as a currency pair.

  • Base currency (left side of the pair): the currency you are buying or selling.
  • Quote currency (right side of the pair): the currency used to price the base currency.
Example — Reading EUR/USD

EUR/USD = 1.0850 means: 1 Euro costs 1.0850 US Dollars. If you buy EUR/USD, you're buying Euros and simultaneously selling Dollars. If the pair moves to 1.0950, Euros became more expensive — you made 100 pips profit.

The Major Pairs

The "majors" are the most liquid, lowest-spread currency pairs — all priced against the US Dollar:

  • EUR/USD — Euro / US Dollar (most traded pair globally)
  • USD/JPY — US Dollar / Japanese Yen
  • GBP/USD — British Pound / US Dollar (nicknamed "Cable")
  • USD/CHF — US Dollar / Swiss Franc
  • AUD/USD — Australian Dollar / US Dollar

Pips — The Unit of Forex Movement

A pip (Percentage in Point) is the standardised unit of price movement in forex. For most pairs (priced to 4 decimal places), 1 pip = 0.0001.

Example — Counting Pips

EUR/USD moves from 1.0850 to 1.0875. That's a move of 0.0025 = 25 pips. If you're trading a standard lot (100,000 units), each pip is worth approximately $10. So a 25-pip move = $250 profit (or loss).

USD/JPY is the major exception — priced to 2 decimal places, so 1 pip = 0.01 for that pair.

What Moves Currency Prices?

Currency exchange rates are driven by several overlapping forces:

Interest Rates

The most powerful driver. Higher interest rates attract foreign capital (investors seeking better returns), increasing demand for that currency and pushing its value up. Central bank decisions — the US Federal Reserve, European Central Bank, Bank of England — are the single most market-moving events in forex.

Economic Data

GDP growth, inflation (CPI), unemployment, and retail sales data all signal the health of an economy. Strong data typically strengthens a currency; weak data weakens it.

Political Stability

Elections, political crises, and geopolitical tensions can cause sharp currency moves. The British Pound dropped roughly 10% overnight when the UK voted for Brexit in 2016 — one of the largest single-day moves in forex history.

Tip: Watch the Economic Calendar

Major economic data releases cause sudden, large price moves in forex. As a beginner, either avoid trading during these events or widen your stop-loss to account for the volatility spike. Most trading platforms provide an economic calendar showing scheduled release times.
