The Stock Market

The stock market is the most widely discussed financial market in the world. It's where companies raise capital by selling ownership stakes, and where millions of investors and traders buy and sell those stakes every day. Understanding how it works is foundational to almost every trading strategy.

What is a Stock?

A stock (also called a share or equity) represents fractional ownership of a company. When a company wants to raise money to grow — build factories, hire staff, develop products — it can sell shares to the public through an Initial Public Offering (IPO).

Once publicly listed, anyone can buy or sell those shares on the stock exchange. As a shareholder, you own a percentage of the company's assets and are entitled to a proportional share of its profits (paid as dividends).

Key Term: Dividend

A portion of a company's profits paid out to shareholders, usually quarterly. Not all companies pay dividends — high-growth tech companies typically reinvest profits rather than distribute them. Dividends provide passive income on top of any price appreciation.

Example — What Stock Ownership Means

If Apple has 15 billion shares outstanding and you own 1,000 shares, you own 1,000/15,000,000,000 = 0.0000067% of Apple. Small, but it's real ownership — including voting rights on major company decisions at shareholder meetings.

Stock Exchanges

Stocks are bought and sold on organised exchanges. The most important global exchanges include:

  • NYSE (New York Stock Exchange) — the world's largest exchange by market cap. Home to Berkshire Hathaway, JPMorgan, Walmart.
  • NASDAQ — predominantly technology companies. Home to Apple, Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta.
  • London Stock Exchange (LSE) — major European exchange. Home to BP, HSBC, Shell.
  • Tokyo Stock Exchange (TSE) — largest in Asia. Home to Toyota, Sony, SoftBank.

US markets trade Monday–Friday, 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM Eastern Time. Extended pre-market and after-hours sessions exist but have lower liquidity and wider spreads.

Stock Market Indices

An index tracks the combined performance of a specific group of stocks. When you hear "the market is up 1% today," the speaker is usually referring to a major index.

  • S&P 500 — tracks the 500 largest US public companies. The primary benchmark for US market performance.
  • Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) — tracks just 30 major US companies. The oldest and most famous index, though considered less representative than the S&P 500 today.
  • NASDAQ Composite — tracks all stocks listed on NASDAQ, heavily weighted towards technology.
  • FTSE 100 — tracks the 100 largest companies on the London Stock Exchange.
Tip: Index as a Benchmark

Professionals use indices to benchmark their performance. If you're up 10% but the S&P 500 is up 15%, you've actually underperformed the market. Beating a major index consistently is extremely difficult — which is why many experts recommend just investing in index funds.

Market Capitalisation

Market capitalisation (market cap) is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares: Market Cap = Share Price × Total Shares.

  • Large-cap (over $10 billion): Stable, established companies. Lower risk, lower growth potential. Examples: Apple (~$3 trillion), Microsoft.
  • Mid-cap ($2–10 billion): Growing companies with more upside but higher risk than large-caps.
  • Small-cap (under $2 billion): Smaller companies, often earlier stage. Higher risk/reward ratio, and less analyst coverage.
Example — Calculating Market Cap

A company has 500 million shares outstanding. Its current share price is $40. Market cap = 500,000,000 × $40 = $20 billion (large-cap). If the price rises to $50, market cap becomes $25 billion — the company became $5 billion "more valuable" in the market's eyes.
