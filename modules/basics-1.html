What is Trading?

Trading is the act of buying and selling financial assets — stocks, currencies, commodities, or cryptocurrencies — with the goal of profiting from price changes. Before you can develop any strategy or pick any market, you need to understand what trading actually is and how money is made (or lost).

Defining Trading

At its simplest, trading means exchanging one thing for another. In financial markets, you exchange money for assets — and later exchange those assets back into (hopefully more) money. Unlike running a business that creates products, a trader profits purely from price movement.

If you believe the price of an asset will rise, you buy it (called going "long"). You then sell it later at a higher price, pocketing the difference. If you believe it will fall, you can "short sell" — borrowing an asset, selling it at the current price, then buying it back cheaper.

Key Term: Long Position

Buying an asset with the expectation that its price will rise. You profit when you sell at a higher price than you paid. The maximum loss on a long position is 100% (if the asset goes to zero).

Key Term: Short Selling

Borrowing an asset and selling it, then buying it back later at a (hopefully) lower price. Profit = sell price minus buy-back price. Short selling carries unlimited risk in theory — a price can rise indefinitely.

Types of Traders

Traders are categorized primarily by how long they hold their positions:

Scalpers

Hold positions for seconds to minutes. Aim to capture tiny price moves, making dozens or hundreds of trades per day. Requires extreme focus and fast execution.

Day Traders

Open and close all positions within the same trading day. Never hold overnight. Profit from intraday price swings, typically using technical analysis and charts.

Swing Traders

Hold positions for days to weeks. Aim to capture the "swings" in a trend without the intensity of day trading. Suits people with jobs or other commitments.

Position Traders (Investors)

Hold for months to years. Closer to investing than trading. Focus more on fundamental analysis — the underlying value and growth prospects of an asset.

Tip for Beginners

Most beginners should start with longer time frames (swing trading or investing) before attempting day trading. The slower the time frame, the more time you have to think — and mistakes are far less costly.

How Profit and Loss Work

Profit or loss in trading comes from the difference between your entry price and exit price, multiplied by the number of units you traded.

Example — Long Trade

You buy 100 shares of a company at $50 per share (total cost: $5,000). The price rises to $60. You sell: 100 × $60 = $6,000. Your profit = $6,000 − $5,000 = $1,000 (20% return).

Example — Losing Trade

You buy 100 shares at $50. The price falls to $42. You decide to cut your loss and sell: 100 × $42 = $4,200. Your loss = $4,200 − $5,000 = −$800. Cutting losses quickly is a core skill.

Real trading also involves costs: commissions (fees per trade), spreads (the gap between buy and sell price), and sometimes overnight financing charges if you hold leveraged positions. These costs must be accounted for when calculating true profitability.

Trading vs. Investing

The terms are often used interchangeably, but they describe different approaches:

  • Trading is more frequent, shorter-term, and relies heavily on technical analysis and market timing.
  • Investing is longer-term (years or decades), focuses on fundamental value, and typically involves buying and holding through market fluctuations.
  • Both require discipline and risk management — but the skills, mindset, and tools differ significantly.

Neither approach is inherently superior. Many people combine both: investing the bulk of their savings while trading a smaller portion of their capital for more active returns.
