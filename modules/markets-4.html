Commodities & Gold

Commodities are the raw materials the global economy runs on — the oil in your car, the wheat in your bread, the copper in your phone. They're one of the oldest forms of trade in human history, and today they form a multi-trillion dollar financial market that every serious trader should understand.

What are Commodities?

A commodity is a raw material or primary agricultural product that is interchangeable with other units of the same type. A barrel of Brent crude oil from one supplier is functionally identical to a barrel from another — this uniformity (fungibility) is what makes commodities tradeable on a global scale.

Commodities are traded on specialist exchanges: the Chicago Mercantile Exchange (CME) for many US commodities, the London Metal Exchange (LME) for metals, and the Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) for energy and agricultural products.

Key Term: Fungibility

The property of an asset where individual units are interchangeable. One ounce of gold is identical to any other ounce of gold (of the same purity). This contrasts with stocks, where shares in Company A are not the same as shares in Company B.

Hard vs. Soft Commodities

Commodities are broadly divided into two categories:

Hard Commodities

Mined or extracted from the earth. Their supply is relatively fixed in the short term — you can't grow more gold in a good season.

  • Precious metals: Gold (XAU), Silver (XAG), Platinum, Palladium
  • Base metals: Copper, Aluminium, Zinc, Nickel
  • Energy: Crude Oil (Brent, WTI), Natural Gas, Coal

Soft Commodities

Grown or farmed — so their supply fluctuates with weather, seasons, and agricultural conditions.

  • Grains: Wheat, Corn (Maize), Soybeans, Rice
  • Other agricultural: Coffee, Sugar, Cotton, Cocoa
  • Livestock: Live Cattle, Lean Hogs

Why Gold is Special

Gold is unique among commodities. It has been used as a store of value for over 5,000 years. While other commodities are consumed (oil is burned, wheat is eaten), most gold ever mined still exists above ground — stored in vaults, jewellery, and electronics.

Safe-Haven Asset

When uncertainty hits — wars, financial crises, pandemics — investors flee to gold. During the 2008 financial crisis, while stock markets crashed 50%+, gold rose approximately 25%. During COVID-19 in 2020, gold hit all-time highs as equities collapsed.

Inflation Hedge

Gold tends to hold its purchasing power over time. When central banks print money (expanding the money supply), each dollar buys less — but gold, being a physical asset with limited supply, tends to retain value. This is why central banks worldwide hold gold in their reserves.

Example — Gold as Portfolio Insurance

A classic portfolio diversification technique: hold 5–10% of a portfolio in gold. When equities fall sharply (as they often do in recessions), gold typically rises or holds steady — reducing overall portfolio losses. Gold and stocks have historically had a low or negative correlation.

Futures Contracts

Most professional commodity trading happens through futures contracts rather than buying the physical commodity.

Key Term: Futures Contract

A legal agreement to buy or sell a specific quantity of a commodity at a predetermined price on a specified future date. Example: an oil futures contract might commit to buying 1,000 barrels of crude at $80/barrel in three months.

Futures serve two main purposes:

  • Hedging: A farmer growing wheat can lock in today's price for their harvest three months from now, protecting against a price drop. An airline can lock in a fuel price months ahead to manage costs.
  • Speculation: Traders buy and sell futures contracts to profit from price movements without ever wanting the physical commodity. Most retail commodity traders close their positions before the delivery date.
Tip: You Don't Have to Trade Futures

Retail traders can access commodity price movements through CFDs (Contracts for Difference), ETFs (like gold ETFs), or commodity stocks (mining companies, energy producers) without dealing with the complexity of futures expiry dates and physical delivery.
