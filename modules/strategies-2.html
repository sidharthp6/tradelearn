Day Trading

Day trading is the most intense form of active trading — all positions are opened and closed within the same trading day. It demands focus, discipline, fast decision-making, and a deep understanding of market structure. This module covers what day trading actually involves, the tools traders use, and a frank look at the risks.

What is Day Trading?

The defining rule of day trading: no positions are held overnight. Every trade opened during market hours is closed by the end of the same session. This eliminates overnight risk — the possibility that news breaks while markets are closed and gaps your position significantly against you.

Day traders aim to profit from intraday price movements — which are often small. To make meaningful returns from small moves, many day traders use leverage (borrowing capital from their broker to control larger positions). Leverage amplifies both profits and losses.

Key Term: Leverage

Leverage allows you to control a position larger than your capital. 10:1 leverage means $1,000 of your money controls a $10,000 position. A 1% move in the asset equals a 10% change in your account. Leverage magnifies gains — but also magnifies losses proportionally.

Technical Analysis — The Day Trader's Primary Tool

Day traders cannot rely on fundamental analysis (company earnings, economic reports) for split-second decisions. Instead they rely on technical analysis — reading price charts to identify patterns, momentum, and likely future direction.

Candlestick Charts

Each "candle" on a chart shows four data points for a time period: Open, High, Low, and Close. The body shows where price started and ended; the wicks show the extremes. Candlestick patterns (like doji, hammer, or engulfing candles) signal potential reversals or continuations.

Moving Averages

A moving average smooths out price data to show the average price over a set period (e.g., the 20-period MA shows the average closing price of the last 20 candles). When price crosses above a moving average, it's often a bullish signal; crossing below is bearish.

Volume

Volume measures how many units of an asset were traded in a period. High volume validates a price move — it means many participants agree. A large price move on low volume is suspect and may not hold.

Example — Reading a Basic Setup

A stock opens strongly, pulls back to its 20-period moving average on declining volume (less selling pressure), then resumes upward on high volume. A day trader sees this as a "pullback to support" setup — buying the dip on the moving average with a stop-loss just below it.

Common Day Trading Approaches

Momentum Trading

Trading stocks or assets that are moving strongly in one direction on high volume — the idea being that momentum tends to continue in the short term. Common at market open when overnight news creates strong directional moves.

Breakout Trading

Entering a position when price breaks above a resistance level (or below a support level) with conviction. The theory: once a key level is broken, the move often accelerates as new buyers pile in and stop-loss orders from shorts are triggered.

Scalping

Making dozens to hundreds of trades per day, each targeting very small price moves (a few ticks or pips). Scalpers rely on high volume and tight spreads, and need fast execution technology. It is the most demanding form of trading psychologically and technically.

The Reality of Day Trading

Day trading is glamorised in social media, but the statistics are sobering:

  • Multiple academic studies estimate that 70–80% of retail day traders lose money over any sustained period.
  • Transaction costs (spreads, commissions, overnight financing on leveraged positions) erode profits significantly on small moves.
  • You are competing against algorithmic systems that execute in microseconds with no emotional bias.
Tip: Start with Paper Trading

Most platforms offer a simulated "paper trading" or "demo" account where you trade with virtual money at real market prices. Spend at least 2–3 months paper trading before risking real capital. If you can't be consistently profitable on paper, you won't be with real money — but if you can, you'll have built confidence and a tested process.

This isn't meant to discourage you — but to set realistic expectations. Day trading is a learnable craft. Many successful day traders spent 1–2 years learning before they became profitable. The key is starting small, cutting losses quickly, and treating every loss as a learning opportunity.
