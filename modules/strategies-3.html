Swing Trading

Swing trading sits in the sweet spot between the intensity of day trading and the patience of long-term investing. By holding positions for days to weeks, swing traders capture meaningful price "swings" within a trend — without needing to watch charts all day. It's considered one of the most approachable strategies for beginners with limited time.

What is Swing Trading?

Swing traders hold positions for anywhere from two days to several weeks. The goal is to capture a single directional price "swing" — buying at the bottom of a pullback in an uptrend, or selling at the top of a rally in a downtrend.

Unlike day trading, swing traders don't need to monitor charts in real time. A typical routine involves analysing charts in the evening, placing orders before the next session opens, then checking in briefly during the day. This makes it compatible with having a full-time job or other commitments.

Key Term: Price Swing

A directional price move that lasts several days. Within any trend, prices don't move in a straight line — they advance, pull back, then advance again. Each individual advance or decline is a "swing." Swing traders aim to enter at the start of a swing and exit near its end.

Support and Resistance

Support and resistance are the most fundamental concepts in all of technical analysis — and swing trading revolves around them.

Support

A price level where buying pressure has historically been strong enough to stop a price from falling further. Think of it as a "floor" — prices bounce up from support. When an asset pulls back to a support level, swing traders see a potential entry point for a long trade.

Resistance

A price level where selling pressure has historically been strong enough to stop a price from rising further. Think of it as a "ceiling" — prices bounce down from resistance. When an asset rallies to resistance, swing traders may exit longs or enter short trades.

Example — Role Reversal

A stock repeatedly fails to break above $80 (resistance). Eventually it breaks through on high volume. Now, $80 becomes support — the "old ceiling becomes a new floor." This role reversal is one of the most powerful and reliable concepts in technical analysis, and swing traders specifically look for these levels to trade from.

Tip: Confluence Makes Levels Stronger

A support level is more reliable when multiple factors align at the same price — e.g., a previous swing low, a moving average, and a round number (like $50 or $100) all at the same level. The more factors that point to the same price, the stronger the support or resistance is likely to be.

Following the Trend

"The trend is your friend" is the oldest maxim in trading — and swing trading is fundamentally a trend-following strategy.

Identifying an Uptrend

An uptrend consists of a series of higher highs and higher lows. Each peak is higher than the last; each pullback bottom is also higher than the last. In an uptrend, swing traders look for opportunities to buy during pullbacks to support, targeting the next high.

Identifying a Downtrend

A downtrend consists of lower highs and lower lows. In a downtrend, swing traders look for opportunities to sell (or short) during rallies to resistance, targeting the next low.

The fundamental mistake beginners make: trying to "catch the turn" — buying a falling asset because it looks cheap, or selling a rising asset because it looks expensive. Trends persist far longer than most people expect. Until the trend structure actually changes (e.g., the series of higher lows is broken), trade with the trend, not against it.

Swing Trading vs. Day Trading

Both are active trading strategies, but they differ in several important ways:

  • Time commitment: Swing trading requires far less screen time — hours per week vs. hours per day for day trading.
  • Trade frequency: Swing traders make fewer trades (a few per week vs. dozens per day), meaning lower transaction costs as a percentage of returns.
  • Stress level: Without the need for split-second decisions, swing trading is significantly less stressful and allows for calmer, more thorough analysis.
  • Overnight risk: Swing traders hold through overnight sessions — news can cause gaps. This is managed through appropriate position sizing and stop-losses (usually wider than day trading stops).
  • Learning curve: Most experienced traders recommend beginners start with swing trading before attempting day trading.
