Risk Management

Risk management is the single most important skill in trading — more important than any entry strategy, indicator, or market selection. Every professional trader thinks about how much they could lose before they think about how much they could gain. This module explains why, and how.

Why Risk Management Comes First

Most beginners focus entirely on finding winning trades. Professionals focus on surviving losing trades — because losing trades are inevitable for everyone, no matter how good you are.

Consider this: a strategy that wins only 40% of the time can be very profitable if the wins are 3× larger than the losses. Conversely, a strategy with an 80% win rate can still be ruinous if the rare losses wipe out all previous gains.

Good risk management means you stay in the game long enough for your edge to play out over many trades.

Key Term: Drawdown

The peak-to-trough decline in your account balance during a losing streak. A 20% drawdown requires a 25% gain to recover. A 50% drawdown requires a 100% gain. This asymmetry is why keeping drawdowns small is so critical.

Position Sizing — The 2% Rule

Position sizing answers: "How much money should I put into this trade?" The answer should never be "as much as possible."

The industry-standard guideline for beginners: never risk more than 1–2% of your total account on any single trade. "Risk" means the maximum amount you're prepared to lose if the trade goes completely wrong.

Example — Applying the 2% Rule

Account balance: $10,000. Maximum risk per trade (2%): $200. You buy a stock at $50 with a stop-loss at $46 (risk = $4 per share). Maximum shares to buy: $200 ÷ $4 = 50 shares (total position: $2,500). Even if this trade is a total loss, you only lose 2% of your account.

With a 2% rule, you can absorb 20 consecutive losing trades and still have 66% of your account. Psychologically, this also prevents panic — no single trade can ruin you.

Stop-Loss Orders

A stop-loss is an automatic order that closes your trade if the price moves against you to a specified level. It is your safety net — it puts a hard cap on how much any single trade can cost you.

Key Term: Stop-Loss

An order placed with your broker to automatically sell (or buy back, in a short) a position once the price reaches a certain level. It removes the emotion from loss-cutting — the trade closes automatically, no hesitation required.

The golden rule: set your stop-loss level before you enter the trade. Once you're in a losing trade, emotions (hope, denial) make it extremely difficult to cut losses rationally. Pre-planning removes that decision from the heat of the moment.

Tip: Don't Move Your Stop-Loss Against the Trade

A common beginner mistake is moving the stop-loss further away when a trade moves against them, to "give it more room." This is almost always a rationalisation of hope. Your stop-loss is where you've decided you're wrong — honour it.

The Risk/Reward Ratio

The risk/reward ratio (R:R) compares how much you stand to lose versus how much you stand to gain on a trade. It is written as 1:X where X is the reward multiple.

  • 1:1 — risk $100 to potentially make $100. Break-even requires 50% win rate.
  • 1:2 — risk $100 to potentially make $200. Profitable with just 34% win rate.
  • 1:3 — risk $100 to potentially make $300. Profitable with just 26% win rate.
Example — Why R:R Matters

You take 10 trades with a 1:2 risk/reward, risking $100 each time. You win 5 and lose 5. Wins: 5 × $200 = $1,000. Losses: 5 × $100 = $500. Net profit: +$500 — despite a 50% win rate. This is why the R:R ratio is as important as your win percentage.

Professional traders typically seek a minimum 1:2 R:R on every trade. Some aim for 1:3 or higher. Anything below 1:1 should be avoided unless your win rate is exceptionally high.
